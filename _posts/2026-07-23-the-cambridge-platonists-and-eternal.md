George Rust, A Letter of Resolution Concerning Origen Introduction Okay, this essay is a little bit of a mishmash. I am first going to introduce my own personal objections to eternal hell (which I think are pretty typical objections) and then discuss the history and soteriology of a seventeenth century Cambridge Platonist, George Rust. I present these together because I think it is sort of fun to compare and see the similarities, and to learn a bit of history. My blog is sometimes pretty interesting. An Objection or Two to Eternal Hell This section discusses my personal big moral-philosophical problem with Hell. It\u2019s basically just moral intuitionism. I am not a Christian, and like many non-Christians (and Christians alike!) I find the notion of damnation morally unappealing. 1 \u201cHell\u201d in most modern Christian traditions refers to a doctrine called Eternal Conscious Torment (ECT or infernalism), where the soul is tormented forever by both \u201cthe pains of hell, \u2018eternal fire\u2019\u201d and their own separation from God. This punishment is prescribed for those who die without repenting of mortal sins, which include things like being an unrepentant atheist or cheating on your spouse. 2 On this soteriological view, lots of people who have saved many lives, been loving parents, and lived selflessly are suffering eternal and pointless torment! Imagine the following case: John, a 22-year-old baptized Catholic firefighter has a valid, sacramental marriage to a Catholic woman. They are unhappily married for 6 months, at which point she leaves him. They never divorce or annul their marriage. John spends the next 60 years completely abstinent. He saves dozens of lives due to his unusual bravery and selflessness, and upon his retirement he establishes a Catholic orphanage with his life savings. When John is 83, however, he is overcome with anger at the God who has made him live abstinently, and he intentionally and with full knowledge sleeps with a woman. The next day, John feels very ill. He does not consider calling a priest or feel contrition for his actions, and he dies. Presumably, your intuition tells you that John does not deserve Eternal Conscious Torment, and that it would be morally wrong to subject him to such a punishment. As a rule the Church will not declare anyone damned (in part because it concerns the \u201cinterior last act\" and God\u2019s grace which are both unknowable), but because John freely rejected his friendship with God the prognosis is not good. A good Catholic will have a very hard time suggesting that John is not probably damned. 3 Now there are lots of apologetics for why ECT might be justified. Ethan Muse offers a decent defense here (see footnote for summary.) 4 Naturally though, any case for infernalism must bite the bullet and admit that our moral intuitions are wildly wrong. The notion that John will very probably face eternal torment and deprivation from the source of all good for his emotions feels improbably wrong. Nevertheless hardline defenders of the doctrine will insist that John has freely chosen hell, and that our moral intuitions simply do not matter here. More cautious apologists who are somewhat uncomfortable with the doctrine of eternal torment emphasize that the core of ECT is permanent and freely chosen eternal separation from God. Modern apologists will sometimes describe permanent separation from God as if it isn\u2019t a pain worse than any conceivable form of torture multiplied by a billion! 5 I would much rather live in Matthew 25:41\u2019s \u201ceverlasting fire prepared for the devil\u201d than be deprived of any goodness. Just as importantly, the free choice of hell would be a lot more convincing if one could change their choice once they experience it. Instead, most Christians adhere to some version of particular judgment, which suggests that your damnation or salvation is fixed upon the moment of your death. God makes a creature, knowing exactly what it will become and where it will end. If the end is unending misery, then God made something whose entire existence is a net harm to itself. It may well be the case that the individual chooses its rejection of God through free will, but the being did not will itself into existence. God did, and he did it seeing the whole of it in advance: every good that creature would ever enjoy, and the misery that would outweigh all of it. Compassion, one would think, declines to make such a thing. So the question is not only whether the damned deserve what they get, but why a good God, foreseeing all, brought them into being at all. And grant too that nobody is damned who did not choose it: the choice still comes second, after God had already settled which creatures there would be, knowing what each of them would choose. So yeah, there are a lot of good arguments against infernalism and really just a handful of serviceable defenses. There are some majorly weird consequences of it too, like lots of religions and sects just overestimating how generous and merciful an omnibenevolent God is. The scriptural case, by the way, is not nearly as strong as most infernalists think . The Cambridge Platonists Here is the fun part: the Cambridge Platonists were a loose group of historians, theologians, and philosophers at Cambridge in the seventeenth century. While there is debate as to what (if any) 6 ideas unified these men, the traditional account attributes to them a rejection of scholasticism, an interest in the new science, and perhaps above all a commitment to the ideas of Plato and neoplatonism. At least for a specific circle within the Cambridge Platonists, Origen was a key figure \u2014 he was a third century neoplatonist Christian theologian, and he famously rejected infernalism. Origen wrote \u201cWe think that the goodness of God, through the mediation of Christ, will bring all creatures to one and the same end\u201d (De Principiis I.6.1-3). A number of Cambridge Platonists read Origen and agreed. I am going to look at A Letter of Resolution Concerning Origen by George Rust, from 1661. The contents of this book were grave and controversial; Rust hid his authorship from his own tutor and fellow Platonist Henry More. Rust begins by introducing the six supposed heresies of Origen, one of which is \u201cThat after long periods of time the damned shall be delivered from their torments, and try their fortunes again in such regions of the world as their nature fits them for.\u201d Rust sets out to defend this principle: God\u2019s eternal mind makes things out of a \u201cprinciple of infinite love,\u201d and he is capable of seeing the probable (or certain?) futures of his creations. If the future of these creations \u201cwas like to be infinitely more sharp or dolorous than all the good they should enjoy from Him\u2026his great compassion certainly would have persuaded him quickly to annihilate them; or rather his Wisdom would have judg\u2019d it more decorous never to have made them.\u201d God is infinitely loving, and has no need of humans. He therefore would not have created any humans for whom the pain of infinite punishment would outweigh the good of their lives. (71-2) The punishments faced in hell are \u201cCurative and for the Emendation of the party suffering; but this if it be eternal in the Scholastic sense of the word leaves no place for the bettering of the sufferers, who are never to get out of this inexplicable Labyrinth of woe and misery.\" This is a rich sentence: the ideal of justice as a restorative is Platonic, and ultimately punishment in Plato\u2019s Gorgias is a sort of paradox. The punished party benefits as their soul is cleansed \u2014 justice reforms us and makes us more just. 7 The dig at the scholastic definition of eternity probably targets Aquinas\u2019 Summa , where Aquinas writes that \u201cThe fire of hell is called eternal, only because it never end [but is does change, so] in hell true eternity does not exist, but rather time (is unending.)\u201d Rust then specifically refers to the idea of unending, not unchanging eternity. (75) Rust defends and modifies Origen\u2019s apokatastasis , a doctrine of universality applied not only to humans in hell but demons and, perhaps, Satan. Rust is hesitant here, and moderates. He suggests that after a very long time the damned get out of their torments, and are then placed wherever suits what they have become. It\u2019s not a promise of restoration to God for all, but it does suggest that there is hope for all the damned. Rust believes that if some souls really are beyond all fixing, then God made creatures he could not repair \u2014 this seems to him an impossibility. Finally, Rust is skeptical that demons cannot be saved, and asks \u201cwhat difference is there in the distance betwixt a devil made an angel, and an angel made a devil?\u201d If devils could be damned, why can they not ultimately be saved? Dante observes the dead laboring in purgatory. 1 I am a theist. 2 The atheist case is a little weird, because Catholic doctrine seems to suggest that knowing an act is wrong is part of the mortal sin.
