feed_content: "Introduction In Oxford, 1555, Hugh Latimer and Nicholas Ridley were burnt to death by Mary I for heresy. According to John Foxe, the two hugged and Ridley told his compatriot to \u201cbe of good heart, brother, for God will either assuage the fury of the flame, or else strengthen us to abide it.\u201d The two men had been tried and sentenced to death for their role in the Edwardian Reformation. Their trial was conducted by disputation \u2013 an oral Latin academic exercise, where one side supported a thesis and the other opposed it through syllogistic logic and appeals to authoritative texts. Catholic theologians served as both their opponents in this disputation and the judges who condemned them to death. Does this story strike you as bizarre? We understand trials to be constructed on the basis of evidence; eyewitness testimony, forensic data, or confessions can all serve as evidence of guilt. We do not accept failure in a rigged disputation as the basis of a death sentence any more than we would accept a poorly written essay defending one\u2019s guilt to be a just basis for imprisoning someone. Even if we were to rig a trial, we would choose to fabricate evidence, pack a jury, or bribe a witness \u2014 not stage a disputation. Thanks for reading! Subscribe for free to receive new posts and support my work. Reading this, you probably understand something implicitly: the processes we consider epistemically valuable change over time. Medieval and early modern Europeans saw some sort of relationship between the disputation and the truth, just as we now see a relationship between scientific inquiry and the truth. This is not to say that we are wrong to make this connection, only that our view of knowledge is socially conditioned and cannot be projected onto the past. Knowledge in History Historians occupy a strange epistemic space, where they both study (directly or indirectly) the social construction of knowledge in the past and work within their own social epistemic paradigm. The problem can be restated as something like this: When we talk about knowledge, we actually mean a particular social phenomenon (regardless of whether true knowledge exists.) 1 This social phenomenon is variable (distinct from true knowledge which is invariable) and the conditions of its production differ across contexts. Societies need some operative knowledge in order to function. In order to establish and perpetuate knowledge, institutions, people, and processes must be endowed with the authority to make truth-claims. Otherwise, why would one believe the Bishop over the Cathar, the upstanding witness over the base criminal, or the biomedical expert over the anti-vaxxer? 2 Historians are also subject to the processes, institutions, and people considered authoritative in their societies. This problem was identified at the beginning of the twentieth century by Karl Mannheim, and built upon by Thomas Kuhn and the Strong Programme in the sociology of science developed at Edinburgh. It is also essential in Foucault, though his work developed independently. Probably the first very successful application of this frame to history came in 1985 with Steven Shapin and Simon Schaffer\u2019s Leviathan and the Air-Pump , which consciously avoided applying modern conceptions of scientific knowledge to the seventeenth century. \u201cSolutions to the problem of knowledge are embedded within practical solutions to the problem of social order.\u201d Leviathan and The Air Pump This is all old hat for the historian or sociologist of science. It has also been applied in various forms and under different names in religious history, book history, legal history, and more. 3 In this post I want to discuss the value of making this idea explicit across historical disciplines, and propose adopting one term in particular from the sociology of knowledge: epistemic authority. This concept will help us answer key questions like what made a claim to knowledge authoritative, and what made certain processes, people, or institutions authoritative sources of it? I think that this framework is not merely useful in one subfield of history but as a paradigm across historical inquiry. What follows is a brief inquiry into epistemic authority in the past, and a case study to demonstrate the concept\u2019s value. Epistemic Authority: Past & Present Christopher J\u00e4ger writes that epistemic authority is the authority we ascribe to people in virtue of their favorable relation to epistemic goods such as true belief, rational credence, knowledge, or understanding...we also talk of non-personal information systems (such as theories, doctrines, traditions, or instruments) having authority. This is a pretty obvious feature of life. We ascribe greater value to truth-claims about diseases made by doctors, and greater value to truth claims about the temperature made by thermometers. In the same vein, we tend to trust the scientific method over even our own sensory experiences. 4 However, it also seems natural that different societies would have different definitions and weights attached to epistemic goods. Rational credence might be paramount in some communities , while others might emphasize true belief and understanding . These societies then will consider different people, institutions, and traditions epistemically authoritative. The same is true more broadly for societies over time. Imagine an encounter between a medieval theologian and a time-traveling modern astronomer. Our astronomer confronts the theologian, and challenges him to a debate on geocentrism. The medieval theologian knows that the earth is the center of the universe; celestial bodies clearly rotate around the earth, it makes more theological sense, and authoritative classical sources like Ptolemy and Aristotle provided suitable mathematical and physical explanations for the motion of the stars based on earth\u2019s centrality. The modern astronomer knows that the earth is not the center of the universe or the solar system; he has advanced telescopes and mathematical models, and he understands Einstein\u2019s special relativity to mean that nothing is at the center of the universe. Importantly, the medieval theologian\u2019s view probably does not change after being confronted with this incontrovertible \u2013 to us \u2013 evidence. To him, the epistemic authority of the sources he has cited is greater than the claims or the evidence of the astronomer. To the astronomer (and the modern historian) the methods of science are authoritative over Ptolemy or the Bible. So, how did we ever come to accept the heliocentric model? Epistemic authority shifts as the operative knowledge societies need change. This is not a move towards skepticism or relativism about knowledge \u2013 our astronomer is certainly right in his assertion. However, the transformation of epistemic authority from the sources trusted by the theologian to the astronomer is the product of identifiable historical processes, and historians must work to identify these processes. For historians to study knowledge fruitfully, we must recognize that our approach is shaped by modern epistemic authorities. After recognizing this, we have to endeavor to treat the causes of true and false beliefs on the same grounds (which the Strong Programme calls symmetry,) without privileging the truth-claims we now credit. This means that when we explain why the theologian believes in the geocentric model or the astronomer the heliocentric, we should search for where they sought epistemic authority. 1 The nature of true knowledge is the domain of epistemologists, and it is hotly contested. Ever since Edmund Gettier tore up the \u201cJustified True Belief\u201d account of knowledge, philosophers have wrestled over alternative ways of defining knowledge. 2 To take the modern example, we believe the expert despite not understanding the science because that process and their position is imbued with a socially defined authority. 3 For example, in Ann Blair\u2019s Too Much to Know, Susan Schreiner\u2019s Are You Alone Wise?, Barbara Shapiro\u2019s A Culture of Fact: England, 1550-1720, and many others. 4 Many examples exist, here are a few: color seems to be in objects, but is in fact a relationship between light and reflective properties; objects seem to be continuous, but are in fact mostly empty space between the atoms which comprise them."
