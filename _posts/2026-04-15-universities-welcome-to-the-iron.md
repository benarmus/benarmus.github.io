feed_content: "Yale employs over 5,460 administrators for fewer than 5,000 undergraduates. Washington University in St. Louis reports 17,019 students and 17,128 non-instructional staff. Harvard employs three administrators for every faculty member. These are not aberrations \u2013 they are the norm in American higher education. Our universities have become an institution driven not by education, but by self-management. Members of the university, welcome to the iron cage . Your institution was once a corporation of professors and students, who worked at and attended their institutions in order to research, teach, and learn. These universities have also always required administrators to handle secondary functions like providing housing, managing salaries and health services, and running a registrar\u2019s office. But as this bureaucracy has expanded, it has become the tail that wags the dog. These administrations which were once secondary have taken over the governance, the priorities, and increasingly the culture of their institutions. Strategic decisions are made by administrators and budgets are shaped by student life, branding, and capital projects. David Bromwich wrote that the universities clearly now [want] to be known not only as a place for teaching, learning, and research, but also as a home, a community, an innovative corporate entity\u2026the swollen self-image requires expanded oversight, and administrators are the overseers. Why is this a crisis? For one, the collapse of public trust in universities is both documented ( very well documented ) and painfully obvious. But more crucially, the universities have lost their identity. In an effort to be everything to everyone, it is no longer clear what a university is. But fear not, academics. Still your itching pen; calm your impulse to write another article for the Chronicle of Higher Education! There might be a solution. But its going to hurt. I. Bloat This phenomenon is not completely new. Max Weber, in his lecture \u201c Science as a Vocation ,\u201d recognized that the American university structure was run by those who \u201cmanage its affairs.\u201d To Weber (brace for Marxism) the university was becoming a state capitalist enterprise, with the professor alienated from the means of production. What he could not have anticipated was the sheer scale of the separation. While Weber\u2019s rationalization thesis explains why some bureaucracy became essential in the universities, it does not account for the massive explosion in administrative power and size since around the 1970s, onward. Bureaucracies, once established, develop their own internal imperatives for self-perpetuation and expansion decoupled from the purposes they were created to serve. The university administration grows not out of genuine demand, but because bureaucracies self-perpetuate. Each new office generates new compliance requirements that justify new offices. Each new student flourishing initiative demands institutional resources to make minute changes and takes over more space which could be used for teaching. The numbers are pretty ridiculous . In 1975, colleges employed one administrator for every eighty-four students. Between 1975 and 2005, the number of administrators increased by 85%, and the number of administrative staffers by 240%, while faculty growth simply kept pace with enrollment. The share of total university spending accounted for by instruction decreased from 41% to 29% since 1980. As Benjamin Ginsberg makes clear, this isn\u2019t driven just by government mandates. If it were, bureaucratic growth would be larger at public universities. In fact, the opposite is true: between 1975 and 2005, administrators at public institutions grew by 66%, while at private colleges they grew by 135%. This isn\u2019t exactly what it seems: it\u2019s not just deans and directors \u2013 it\u2019s also more counselors, more IT staff, more residential life coordinators, more compliance officers, etc. Don\u2019t tell me that this massive staff is necessary to manage a university \u2013 it\u2019s not. European universities run on a fraction of the per-student spending and administrative overhead. Even worse, the core functions of the American university have not changed substantially since the 1960s \u2013 teaching, credentialing, researching, administrating \u2013 all at a fraction of the cost and size. If these universities can function without a Deputy Provost for Holistic Wellness making $150,000 a year, then that position is evidently not load-bearing. Don\u2019t tell me these administrators are necessary to ensure diversity. Our generation did not invent affirmative action: the most diverse period of university expansion in American history, the postwar decades through the 1970s, was accomplished with a fraction of the current DEI infrastructure. The Morrill Acts, the GI Bill, Title VI, and affirmative action transformed the demographics of American higher education. The notion that diversity requires a permanently growing bureaucratic apparatus is a proposition that serves the bureaucracy, not those historically denied access to the academy. Pretty nice office. Jealous, professor? II. Who Let This Happen? There\u2019s a vicious cycle at work here. Since the middle of the twentieth century, universities have necessarily become larger and more complex institutions in order to manage compliance with Title IV, VI, and IX especially. These new obligations beget new administrative offices, which beget new administrative offices, which beget new compliance requirements internal to the institution itself. The spending is all internally justifiable, and it compounds as each department spawns new departments with their own initiatives. These initiatives can be particularly dangerous, as they demand funding for ill-defined goals like \u201cGlobal Perspective,\u201d and \u201cFlourishing.\u201d In large part, university faculty have been willing participants in abdicating power to the bureaucracy. Professors are not trained to administer large and complex institutions, they\u2019re trained to research and write. Committee work is generally thankless. There\u2019s something very seductive about having more time for research and not serving in student conduct investigations. So, they\u2019ve ceded authority to professional administrators and in the process lost much of their power. These administrators don\u2019t deprioritize teaching and research out of malice. They deprioritize them because their careers, networks, and education is in management, not scholarship. Universities have also become fabulously wealthy in the past decades . Because of innovations in endowment management and an influx of donations, even the fairly small public institution of 7,000 students where I study as an undergrad manages 1.6 billion dollars. Bowen\u2019s Law suggests that universities are not cost-minimizing institutions. They maximize revenue and spend to the limit of their intake. They do not set budgets based on education costs and fundraise to meet the budget, they fundraise aggressively and spend what they raise. If faculty controlled budgets, Bowen's Law would predict expanding libraries, smaller class sizes, and generous research funding. The money would still get spent, but it would flow towards the academic mission. But because faculty have abdicated their power, they don\u2019t control budgets. Administrators do. And administrators, quite naturally, spend on what they know and what serves their professional interests. This is why faculty hiring only rises with enrollment, while administrative expenditure rises rapidly. Governing boards have been party to this expansion. University trustees are overwhelmingly drawn from business and finance, and their instincts are to hire managers. When presented with a new administrative office, their default is approval, because organizational growth is legible to them in a way that faculty self-governance isn\u2019t. This governing structure reflexively validates administrative expansion while treating faculty authority as quaint at best and obstructive at worst. Boards measure success through metrics like rankings, surveys, and acceptance rates, all of which are managed administratively. As a result, the language around education has become managerial. Education is a product or a deliverable, and \u2018 strategic plans \u2019 orient policy. When a university describes its mission in terms of 'student success outcomes' rather than the pursuit of knowledge, it has already conceded the argument about what it exists to do. The administrative hydra. III. What can we do? Lest Yale be overrun by an army of administrators, something must be done to slow and eventually reverse the creep of bureaucracy. Ginsberg has suggested in his Fall of the Faculty (which was a major influence on this article) that faculty hate administrative responsibilities because they feel like their administrative bodies have no power. He writes that The RPI faculty senate was disbanded by a provost who did not like its composition and\u2026the Brandeis senate was ignored when it objected to the administration\u2019s violation of university rules. (173) Professors need to recognize, as I think many do, that the fate of the universities is at risk. They still have enough structural power to recapture authority and remind these institutions why they exist. That means using collective action (or unions where they exist,) their influence with students, and accreditation bodies. Professors have the power to speak out against their own institutions \u2013 reputation still matters, and it comes from faculty. Ginsberg suggests that faculty should demand seats on the Board of Trustees, and he is right. This is the great fear of the administration \u2013 a direct line between faculty and the board. These professors might disrupt whatever picture of the university the administration has decided to paint, and redirect the distribution of funds. Professors should also seek to reform (or where they exist, actively advocate for) their senates. By placing pressure on the university, faculty should demand that senates be granted binding authority over academic appointments, curricular decisions, and the creation of new administrative positions. A senate that can only advise is a senate that can be ignored, and if it can be ignored it will be ignored. Though these mechanisms, faculty should protect their control over the curriculum and degree requirements. They should aim for control (with fewer bureaucratic intermediaries) over hiring, promotion, and tenure. They should even push for oversight in undergraduate(!) admissions. Academic discipline should become the exclusive purview of faculty. Most importantly, professors need authority over budget priorities, like the ratio of tenure lines to adjunct and staff positions. If these goals seem absurd, it\u2019s because they are ambitious. And university faculty have not been collectively ambitious for a long time. Both students and professors will have to change their priorities in this new model. Students will have to demand less by way of resources and accommodations, and more by way of educational quality. Professors (especially senior faculty) will have to take on significant commitments that will be boring and time-consuming in order to recapture the destiny of their institutions. But ask yourself: what is this all for? Why has our civilization constructed this elaborate, multi-trillion dollar edifice? The answer once seemed obvious. Universities existed for the discovery, preservation, and transmission of knowledge. This purpose justified public trust, an epistemic authority, academic freedom, and state-favor. This mission is no longer at the forefront. It is buried under layers and layers of bureaucracy and strategic initiatives. Universities have not lost the public trust, they have abdicated it. Walking across a campus you will no longer hear students singing, as thirteenth century German students did , \u201cLong live the university, long live the professors. Long live each student, long live the student body; forever may they flourish.\u201d This argument is not novel \u2013 at times it has even been trendy. But that is no reason to ignore it. It will bare repeating until something has been done. In this article I have outlined what I think went wrong at the universities, and what we need to remember: the professors and students are the university; the rest is overhead.
